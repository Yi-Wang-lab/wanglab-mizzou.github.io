Research

We study how energy fields such as ultrasonic vibration, electric fields, and lasers can make manufacturing more precise and more capable, then use those processes to build devices for medicine and smarter systems for industry.

Multifield-assisted micro/nanomanufacturing

Electrohydrodynamic (EHD) printing uses an electric field to pull an ultrafine jet from a nozzle, so it can write features much smaller than the nozzle itself. We combine EHD and extrusion-based printing with ultrasonic vibration to print high-resolution, multi-material, and high-aspect-ratio structures, including metallic 3D microneedle arrays, flexible multilayer circuits, and laser-induced graphene embedded in high-performance polymers such as PEEK.

Our NSF CAREER project investigates ultrasonic vibration-enhanced anti-gravity EHD printing, aiming at high-precision fabrication of functional structures on Earth and in space. We also study ultrasonic-assisted machining, including rotary ultrasonic machining and ultrasonic-assisted EDM for drilling small holes.

Bioelectronics and brain–machine interfaces

Flexible neural probes can record and modulate brain activity with less tissue damage than rigid electrodes, but they are hard to make and hard to implant. We design and fabricate flexible, MRI-compatible neural microprobes, develop insertion methods such as vibration-assisted implantation with bio-dissolvable guides, and integrate multimodal sensors for closed-loop neuromodulation.

We also build wearable and implantable biosensors from laser-induced graphene and conducting polymers, such as dual-gate organic electrochemical transistors that detect dopamine and glutamate. With clinical collaborators, we are developing high-resolution electrode arrays that detect nerve injury during surgery and implantable stimulators that accelerate and monitor nerve healing.

Smart manufacturing: mixed reality, IIoT, and digital twins

We develop human-centered smart manufacturing systems that connect machines, data, and people. Our mixed reality platforms let operators monitor and control CNC machines and 3D printers through holographic interfaces, and our augmented reality digital twins support reconfigurable additive manufacturing systems.

We combine machine vision, physical modeling, and AI for in-process monitoring and inspection, such as melt-penetration prediction in wire arc additive manufacturing and tolerance measurement on chip sockets. With human factors collaborators, we use eye tracking to study how people learn in augmented reality. Dr. Wang also helps lead Mizzou's Industry 4.0 laboratory and certification program, which trains students and working professionals in smart manufacturing.
